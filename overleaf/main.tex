\author{Anonymous authors}

\begin{abstract}
Organizations allocating work among humans, AI systems, and human--AI teams must balance service, cost, limited review capacity, correlated failures, and hard operating requirements. Indiscriminate adoption of an uncertain computational technology creates a second trust problem: a promising candidate is not sufficient evidence that the technology should be invoked. Q-OPS treats quantum computation as an optional expert inside an application-oriented decision architecture. Strong classical methods establish a credible incumbent and characterize remaining headroom; a future pre-execution router may invoke an optional quantum pilot; the frozen post-simulation gate records evidence after that pilot; and quantum-independent checking by the shared classical evaluator preserves fallback. In the frozen study, the quantum branch audits a retained-state distribution and does not replace the incumbent. Among 32 synthetic held-out instances, 9/32 (28.13\%) meet the recorded post-simulation evidence gate. Across all retained-core comparisons, the ratio of exact optimum masses under the $p=2$ and uniform distributions is 6.831$\times$; this is distinct from the matched 128-draw outcomes. The evaluator records zero disallowed-mode violations, while only 27/32 classical incumbents satisfy the stricter composite-feasibility predicate. Earlier C1.0--C1.1 development stages and the frozen depth comparison preserve negative evidence: strong classical controls can remove apparent headroom, and $p=1$ exceeds $p=2$ on 28/32 cases under unequal parameter-search budgets. The present result is therefore statevector-level conditional algorithmic evidence, not real-QPU, wall-clock, scaling, universal, security, QML, or application-level quantum advantage. Q-OPS illustrates a general rule for trustworthy emerging-computation adoption: compare against credible alternatives, invoke selectively, verify through a quantum-independent path, and retain a fallback.
\end{abstract}
